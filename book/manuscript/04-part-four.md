# Chapter 11: It's Not Too Late

Let me guess the sentence you have been carrying while you read this book. It goes something like: "This is beautiful, Chan, but you started at twenty-one. I'm forty-seven."

Sit down with me. We need to talk about time.

Yes, I created my first deal at twenty-one. Do you know what I brought to that deal besides courage? Nothing. No money, no credit, no track record, no network, no judgment. I had never managed a household budget through a recession. I had never read people across a negotiation table, hired a contractor, survived a bad boss, raised a child, or buried a fear. Twenty-one-year-old Chan had exactly two assets: time and nerve.

Now look at yourself honestly, you at thirty-eight or forty-seven or fifty-six, and take the same inventory. You have decades of pattern recognition that no course can teach. You know when a person is lying to you; it took me years and money to learn that. You may have equity in a home, a retirement account, or simply a credit history, all of which are seats at the table that young Chan had to borrow from strangers. You have a network you do not even recognize as a network: coworkers, school parents, church friends, cousins, every one of them a possible partner, teacher, or connection. And you have something younger women rarely have: you know who you are. Do you have any idea how much money that self-knowledge saves?

The math our culture teaches women about age is simply wrong. It counts the years behind you as spent. They are not spent. They are invested, and you are walking around with the compounded returns: judgment, relationships, resilience, and the priceless ability to tell what matters from what glitters.

And the years ahead? Count them properly too. A woman of forty-five may have forty more years. Forty years is not a remnant. It is an entire second lifetime, longer than most empires of industry took to build. If you bought one modest rental property every few years for even a third of it, you would change your family's story permanently. The question was never how many years you have. It is what the next one is for.

I have watched this play out in real women, not theory. The ones who start at forty and fifty often outperform the ones who start at twenty-five, and there are reasons. They waste less time on ego. They do diligence like their life depends on it, because they know exactly what a setback costs. They pick better partners, having survived a few wrong ones in other arenas. Their "why" is not vague ambition; it is specific and burning: this daughter, this retirement, this freedom, this never-again.

There is one more thing I want to dismantle before you turn the page, and it is tender, so I will be gentle. Somewhere under "it's too late" usually lives a quieter sentence: "I don't deserve to start over." Women carry this one especially. We are so practiced at being the resource for everyone else that spending resources on our own becoming feels like theft.

It is not theft. Listen to an immigrant daughter on this. My parents crossed a river in the dark so that the women of this family could do exactly what you are hesitating to do: begin. Every woman who begins, at any age, honours every woman who never got the chance. Your beginning is not selfish. It is the point of all the sacrifice that came before you, whoever made it.

The current does not check your birthdate before it lets you swim. I promise. I have been swimming against it my whole life, and it has never once asked my age.

**Your Turn**

1. Write your real inventory at your current age: judgment, network, capital, credit, skills, self-knowledge. Compare it honestly to what a twenty-one-year-old has. Who would you rather bet on?
2. How many years might you realistically have ahead? Write the number. What could that "second lifetime" hold if you gave it a direction this year?
3. Whose sacrifice does your beginning honour? Write her name. Begin for her if you cannot yet begin for yourself.

# Chapter 12: Begin Where You Are

Every book like this one faces the same danger in its final chapter. You close the cover, feel wonderful for two days, and change nothing. I refuse to let that happen to us. So this chapter is not a send-off. It is a beginning, laid out in six moves, none of which require money, and all of which require you.

**Move one: write your why where you can see it.** Not "financial freedom." Something with faces in it. My why had a hospital room in it, and my parents' hands. Yours might have a daughter's tuition, a mortgage-free sixtieth birthday, the ability to leave, or the ability to stay on your own terms. You will not outwork fear with willpower. You will outwork it with a reason. Get the reason on paper first, everything else second.

**Move two: learn the language, one hour a week.** Real estate, like every world that guards its doors, runs on vocabulary: cashflow, equity, appreciation, amortization, cap rate, joint venture. None of it is difficult. It is just unfamiliar, and unfamiliarity is what gets mistaken for "not for me." One hour a week, a book, a podcast on your commute, a library course. In six months you will follow any conversation. In a year you will notice something shocking: you can tell when the confident man at the front of the room is wrong.

**Move three: audit your six seats.** You did this in Chapter 9 if you did the work; if not, tonight is a fine night. Money, credit, knowledge, time and effort, opportunity-finding, trust. Two lists: what I have, what I need. This turns the fog of "someday" into a shopping list, and shopping lists get filled.

**Move four: find your people.** Nothing about this path requires you to walk it alone, and almost everything gets easier in company. Every city, Calgary very much included, has real estate meetups, women's investing circles, and rooms full of people trading what they know. Walk in. Yes, the first time is uncomfortable. Go anyway, and go twice, because the second visit is when strangers become familiar. And remember what you learned about the only woman in the room: if that is you, wonderful. Nobody will forget you.

**Move five: talk to women who have done it.** Not to pitch. To listen. Buy a coffee for a woman who owns a rental, who flipped a house, who invests with her sister. Ask her what she wishes she had known. Women who have crossed the water almost always turn around to help the next one across; I have built a career on being that woman, and I am far from the only one. One honest hour with someone six steps ahead of you is worth a shelf of theory.

**Move six: do one small brave thing this month.** Not someday. This month. Walk through an open house and let yourself feel what it would mean to be there as an investor. Ask your bank one question you have been avoiding. Attend one meetup. Make one call. The size of the step matters far less than its direction, because motion creates evidence, and evidence is the only thing that ever truly kills fear. The lemonade stand was small. The first deal was one house. Everything begins embarrassingly small, and then it does not stay small.

That is the whole system. No secrets withheld for a seminar. A woman with a why, a growing vocabulary, an honest inventory, a room of people, a mentor's coffee cup, and one brave step a month is not "getting ready" to change her life. She is already changing it, and within a year she will be unrecognizable to her own doubts.

I want to leave you with the picture I keep on the wall of my mind.

A little girl stands at a chain-link fence with a pitcher of lemonade her mother helped her make from almost nothing. On the other side of the fence is a world that has all the money, all the credit, all the confidence, all the things she does not have. She has a paper cup and a decision.

She does not wait for a gate.

She pours through the fence.

Every single thing in my life came through that gap in the wire: the houses, the freedom, the businesses, the full-circle Canada Day with my sister and our daughters. Not because the fence disappeared. Fences never fully disappear, not for women, not for immigrants, not for anyone starting with less. You do not need the fence to disappear. You need a pitcher, a cup, and the nerve to call out to the people walking past.

You have all three. You always did.

Pour.

**Your Turn**

1. Which of the six moves will you make first, and on what date? Write the date. A move without a date is a wish.
2. What is your "one small brave thing" this month? Tell one person about it today, because spoken intentions survive.
3. One year from now, what would you love to write to me about? Write that letter now, in two paragraphs, dated one year ahead. Keep it somewhere safe. I am serious about this one.

# Epilogue: Full Circle

On Canada Day, in Kin Coulee Park, between the music and the crowds, I watched my niece hand a stranger a cup of The Shag and say thank you with her whole face, and for one moment I could see the entire river at once.

My parents, deciding in the dark to leave everything.

A church full of strangers, signing their names for a family they had never met.

Two little girls at a fence, counting coins into a jar.

A hospital room, and a question that would not let me go.

A twenty-one-year-old with no money and no credit, signing her first deal with a shaking hand.

A thirty-year-old checking her mail and realizing, quietly, that she was free.

Two sisters, forty years later, sold out of nitro cold brew and laughing about it, their daughters beside them.

We are all products of the courage of those who came before us. But courage is not only something you inherit. It is something you deposit, every time you begin something scared, for the women who come after you, whether they share your blood or only your hope.

This book was my deposit.

Your move, my friend. The water is cold, the current is real, and you were built for it.

I will see you on the other side of the river.

**Chan**
