# PART TWO: AGAINST THE CURRENT

*Finding your way when the way was not built for you*

# Chapter 4: The Only Woman in the Room

I have a confession: I was a reckless, chaotic student.

Not reckless in the way that ruins lives. Reckless in the way that refuses to sit still inside someone else's plan. If everyone was walking one direction, I felt an almost physical pull to walk the other way, just to see what was over there. For years I thought this was a flaw I needed to fix. It turned out to be the engine of my whole life.

At university, I wanted a challenge. I was ambitious and hungry, so I aimed at one of the hardest programs I could find: chemical engineering. I climbed. I did well. And then, halfway up the mountain, I discovered a terrible secret that nobody warns overachievers about.

I was bored.

Not struggling. Bored. There is a difference, and it took me a long time to respect it. Struggle means you are growing. Boredom means you are dying quietly in a room that looks like success. So I did the thing good immigrant daughters are absolutely not supposed to do.

I dropped out.

I can still feel the weight of that decision. My parents had crossed a river and an ocean so their children could have exactly this kind of education, and here I was walking away from it. But I was not walking away from effort. I was walking toward a bigger fight. I set my eyes on petroleum engineering, an even harder program, and I made it in.

There was one detail about that program that everyone around me saw as a problem: I was the only woman in it.

The only one. Every classroom, every lab, every study group: me and the men.

And here is the part of my story that I need you to hear exactly as I mean it. I loved it. The odds stacked against me were not the price of admission. They were the attraction. I loved being the stone in the current. I loved swimming against the ocean. Where other people saw isolation, I saw distinction. Nobody forgot who I was. Nobody confused me with anyone else. Every room I walked into, I had already done something the men in it had not: I had walked in anyway.

Years later I understood that this was not really about engineering at all. University was a training ground for what came next, because a woman building wealth in the 1990s walked into the only-woman-in-the-room experience over and over. Bankers' offices. Investor meetings. Renovation sites. Negotiation tables. If I had waited for those rooms to look like me before I entered them, I would still be waiting.

So let me hand you the three things being the only woman in the room taught me, because you will need them.

First, being different is not a limitation. It is freedom. When you do not fit the mold, you are excused from the mold. Nobody expects you to do it the standard way, which means you are free to do it your way. From the humblest of beginnings, I can tell you: being different does not make you limited. It gives you the freedom to become anything you want.

Second, discomfort is information, not instruction. The knot in your stomach when you are the only woman at the investors' meetup does not mean "leave." It means "you are somewhere new." Those are not the same message. Learn to tell them apart and half your fear becomes fuel.

Third, boredom is a compass. If you are excellent at something that is slowly putting you to sleep, you are not obligated to keep doing it. Yes, even at forty-five. Especially at forty-five. Quitting the wrong mountain is not failure. It is navigation.

**Your Turn**

1. Where in your life are you succeeding at something that bores you? Be honest. What would "walking toward a bigger fight" look like?
2. Recall a time you were the "only one" in a room: the only woman, the only newcomer, the only one without the credential. What did you do that you are proud of?
3. What rule were you raised to follow that you have secretly outgrown?

# Chapter 5: When Everything Falls Apart

Every woman I have ever coached has a chapter like this one. If you have not lived yours yet, you will, and I say that not to frighten you but to prepare you: the worst season of your life may also be the one that finally sets you in motion.

Mine came when my parents' health collapsed.

They were both in critical condition. We were still a young immigrant family, not long in Canada, without deep savings, without a professional network, without a safety net of any kind. My sister and I looked at each other and understood, without saying it out loud, that the two little girls who sold lemonade through the fence were now responsible for sustaining the family.

I remember the fear. But more than the fear, I remember a kind of terrible clarity. Desperation strips away every polite fiction about how life works. It showed me the truth: I could spend the next forty years trading my hours for dollars, always one crisis away from the edge, exactly as vulnerable as we were at that moment. Or I could find another way.

That was the moment my entrepreneurship journey actually began. Not with a vision board. Not with a seminar. With a hospital, a stack of bills, and a question I could not put down:

How can I build income that does not require me to trade my life away to earn it?

I want to be careful here, because I know who is reading this book. You may be in your own falling-apart season right now. A divorce that rewrote your future. A layoff at fifty. A diagnosis, yours or someone you love. Parents who suddenly need you the way you once needed them. If that is you, I am not going to insult you with "everything happens for a reason." Some things just hurt, and you are allowed to say so.

What I will tell you is what I found on the other side of my season.

I did not want to survive. I wanted to live.

That sentence became the dividing line of my life. Survival is what you do when you accept the terms you were handed. Living is what happens when you renegotiate them. Surviving asked me: how do I cover this month? Living asked me: how do I make sure no month ever owns me again?

Notice what changed. The crisis did not give me resources. We were as broke after my parents got sick as before. What the crisis gave me was a question, and a reason strong enough to carry it. My why was lying in a hospital bed. Everything I built afterward, every property, every business, every risk, traces back to that room.

Here is what I know now, twenty-five years and many storms later. Pain is a terrible master but an extraordinary fuel. I took all of the pain my family had experienced, the war, the camp, the poverty, the hospital, and I used it as the raw material for a future none of it predicted. Not because I am special. Because I finally had a reason bigger than my fear.

Your crisis is asking you a question. Most people only hear the noise. Listen for the question.

**Your Turn**

1. What is, or was, your falling-apart season? Write it down plainly, without softening it.
2. What question is that season asking you? Not "why me," but "what now."
3. Complete this sentence: "I do not want to survive. I want to..." Keep writing until something on the page makes your heart beat faster.

# Chapter 6: No Money, No Credit, No Permission

At twenty-one years old, I put together my first real estate deal: a detached home in Calgary.

I had no money of my own in it. I had no credit. What I had was a problem worth solving, the willingness to learn in public, and the humility to bring other people into what I could not do alone.

Now, this is the part of my story where people usually lean in and ask for the mechanics. What clause? What structure? And there are answers, an entire toolbox of them: seller financing, joint ventures, assumable mortgages, rent-to-own, partnerships where one person brings capital and the other brings the deal and the work. Creative financing is real, it is learnable, and I have spent decades using it and teaching it.

But if I give you the mechanics before the principle, I will have wasted this chapter, because the mechanics change with every market and the principle never does. So here is the principle.

**Every deal is a table with several seats, and you only need to fill one of them.**

A real estate deal needs money. It needs credit. It needs knowledge. It needs time and effort. It needs the deal itself, meaning someone who found the opportunity. And it needs trust, the glue that lets strangers cooperate. Six seats. The lie that stops most women is the belief that they must fill all six seats alone before they are allowed to begin. So they wait. They wait until they have the down payment and the perfect credit score and the confidence, and somewhere in the waiting, a decade quietly disappears.

At twenty-one I could fill exactly two seats. I had found an opportunity, and I was willing to do the work no one else wanted to do. Money? Someone else had that. Credit? Someone else had that too. The question was never "do I have everything?" The question was "who has the rest, and why would they want to sit at a table with me?"

The answer to that second question is the most liberating thing I will tell you in this book: people with money are looking for people with problems solved. Capital is abundant and initiative is rare, which means the person who brings a well-prepared opportunity to the table is not begging. She is offering.

I did not know any of this as theory back then. I learned it by doing it scared. My hands shook. I said "I don't know, but I will find out" more times than I could count. I let people who knew more than me check my work, and some of the correction stung. The deal closed anyway. A girl who grew up in hand-me-downs, whose family had arrived with nothing, controlled a house in Calgary before she could legally rent a car in some countries.

And the strangest thing happened in the weeks after. Nothing outside me changed, but everything inside me did. The world had been divided into people who do these things and people like me. That line turned out to be imaginary. I had drawn it myself, and I could erase it myself.

You have a version of that line. Everyone does. Mine said "girls from refugee camps don't own real estate." Yours might say "women my age don't start over" or "I'm not a numbers person" or "people like me don't ask rich people for anything." Whatever it says, the line is drawn in the same washable ink mine was.

No money is a problem with a hundred known solutions. No credit is a problem with a hundred known solutions. No permission? That one is not even a problem. Nobody was ever going to give it to you anyway. It was always yours to give yourself.

**Your Turn**

1. Look at the six seats: money, credit, knowledge, time and effort, the found opportunity, and trust. Which two can you fill today? Be generous with yourself and honest.
2. Whose "table" could you add real value to right now, even without money?
3. Write out your imaginary line: "People like me don't..." Now write the name of one real woman who did it anyway. If you cannot name one, you have not looked yet. Go look.

# Chapter 7: Paid While You Sleep

By the time I was thirty, something quietly historic had happened in my family, and almost nobody knew about it.

The rental properties I had built and partnered into across Calgary and Saskatoon were paying me more every month than my full-time job. Positive cashflow, month after month, arriving whether I got out of bed or not. In one generation, my family had gone from a refugee camp to owning income that worked while we slept.

I need to tell you what that actually felt like, because the number was never the point.

It felt like exhaling. For the first time in my life, and if I am honest, for the first time in my family's remembered history, the ground was not moving. A job loss could not sink us. A crisis could not undo us the way my parents' illness nearly had. When I walked into my job, I walked in by choice, and everyone can feel the difference between a woman who is there by choice and a woman who is trapped. So can she.

This is what I mean when I say wealth, and I want to define it carefully, because the world has handed women some very silly definitions.

Wealth is not a handbag. It is not a number chosen to impress strangers. Wealth is options. It is the ability to say no: to a bad boss, a bad deal, a bad situation that a paycheque used to force you to tolerate. It is the ability to say yes: to your daughter's opportunity, to your parents' care, to your own rest. Cashflow is simply freedom, paid monthly.

And for women, cashflow is something more. It is safety. I have sat with too many women who stayed too long in jobs and marriages and circumstances that diminished them, not because they could not see the exit, but because they could not afford it. I do not judge a single one of them. I simply want the next generation of women, and I mean you, to hold the key to their own doors.

There is a second thing I learned in those years, and it changed how I see money forever: real estate done right is not extraction. It is service, paid well.

Some of the most meaningful work of my career has been with homeowners facing foreclosure. Families out of options, ashamed, hiding letters from the bank. The banks would not teach them their choices, and the schools never had. I could sit at their kitchen table and show them a way to stop the foreclosure, maximize what their home returned to them, and walk out with dignity instead of being carried out by a process. I made a living. They kept their self-respect and often a meaningful cheque. Both things were true at once, and I stopped apologizing for that a long time ago.

Whatever you were taught, money earned by solving real problems for real people is honourable. A woman with cashflow is not less kind, less feminine, or less devoted to her family. She is more dangerous to every circumstance that used to push her around. That is the whole idea.

One warning before we move on, because I promised you honesty. Passive income is not passive at the beginning. Those properties were bought with years of learning, unglamorous work, tenant calls, and mistakes I paid tuition on. The freedom of my thirties was purchased by the discipline of my twenties. There is no version of this story without the work. There is only the choice of which decade does the paying: an early one, on your terms, or every decade, on someone else's.

1. What would "the ability to say no" change in your life, specifically? Name the first no you would say.
2. Look at your current income. If life paused you for six months, what would keep arriving? What is one step toward changing that answer?
3. What belief do you carry about women and money that you would never allow someone to say about your daughter or your best friend?
