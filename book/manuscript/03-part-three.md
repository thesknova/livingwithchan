# PART THREE: STRONGER TOGETHER

*The power of partnership, and how to find your seat at the table*

# Chapter 8: Never Judge a Book

I have to tell you about a flaw of mine, because overcoming it built my career, and I see the same flaw quietly costing women everywhere.

I used to judge books by their covers. People, I mean. I would take one look and decide who was worth listening to. The polished ones, the credentialed ones, the ones who looked like success as I imagined it: those were the teachers. Everyone else was background.

Life corrected me, repeatedly and without mercy.

Some of the most important lessons of my investing life came from people I had almost dismissed. The landlord in the worn jacket who owned half a street. The quiet woman at the back of the meeting who had renovated more houses than everyone talking at the front. The seller across the kitchen table who taught me more about negotiation by watching him than any course ever did. You can never know a story until you read it yourself. Covers lie in both directions: they hide wisdom, and they dress up noise.

The day I finally learned this was one of the true beginnings of my success, and the lesson has a name. It is called humility, and it is not what most people think.

Humility is not thinking less of yourself. I want to be very clear with you about that, because women have been handed enough smallness for one century. Humility is the discipline of stepping aside, closing your mouth, and opening your ears to people who know more than you and have what you want. That is the whole practice. It costs nothing, it is available to everyone, and it is shockingly rare.

Here is why it is rare: listening feels like losing. When someone corrects your numbers or questions your plan, everything in you wants to defend, explain, prove. I had that instinct in abundance. I was ambitious, an overachiever, allergic to being wrong. Learning to say five simple words, "teach me, I'm listening," felt at first like swallowing stones. It turned out to be swallowing keys.

Because look at what listening actually buys you. Every person who has already walked your road is carrying a map. Most of them, and this surprised me, are happy to share it. People love to teach what they have mastered; they are just tired of teaching people who will not hear them. When you become a woman who genuinely listens, mentors find you. Doors you did not knock on start opening from the inside.

There is a flip side to this chapter, and it belongs to you especially.

You will be judged by your cover too. As a woman, and in my case a female immigrant with an accent and a name nobody could pronounce, you will walk into rooms where someone has already decided your story without reading it. Chan-SU-da. I have watched people's eyes slide past me toward whichever man in the room looked more like their idea of an investor.

I used to resent it. Then I learned to use it.

Being underestimated is a strategic gift. The underestimated woman gets to watch, listen, and learn while everyone else performs. She hears the honest numbers because nobody bothers to posture for her. And when she finally moves, she moves with information the posturers gave away for free. Some of my best deals were built in rooms where I was invisible right up until the moment I wasn't.

So carry both halves of this lesson together. Read others slowly, generously, past their covers, because teachers rarely look like teachers. And when others read you wrongly, do not waste your energy correcting the review. Let them keep their cheap edition of you. The full story is none of their business until it is standing at the closing table.

**Your Turn**

1. Who in your life have you been dismissing by their cover? What might they know that you need?
2. When were you last genuinely corrected, and how did you respond? What would "teach me, I'm listening" cost you? What might it buy?
3. Recall a moment you were underestimated. What information or freedom did it actually hand you, and did you use it?

# Chapter 9: What You Bring to the Table

If I could sit across from you with two cups of tea and ask one question, it would be this: what do you think you bring to the table?

I have asked hundreds of women some version of it. The answers break my heart a little every time. "Honestly, not much yet." "I'm still learning." "I'd mostly need other people to carry me at first." Then I ask the same woman about her life, and within ten minutes she has casually described managing renovations, untangling a parent's estate, running a school fundraiser that raised more than some small businesses earn, negotiating a car purchase, keeping a household solvent through a layoff, and talking a teenager off three separate ledges.

Not much yet.

We established the six seats at every deal's table back in Chapter 6: money, credit, knowledge, time and effort, the found opportunity, and trust. Now I want to show you where women fit at that table, because it is almost never where they assume.

Most women assume their seat is "none, until I have money." But money is the most replaceable seat at the table. I say that as someone who has raised it, borrowed it, and partnered for it: capital is a commodity. It has no ideas, finds no deals, and fixes no problems at two a.m. Every serious investor I know has more capital than good opportunities and trustworthy people to share them with. Read that sentence again, because it is the quiet engine of this whole book.

The scarce seats, the ones people compete to fill, are the human ones. And they happen to be the ones women have been unknowingly training for their entire lives.

**Trust.** Deals are made of promises kept over years. Who gets invited back, again and again, is the person others can rely on. Women build and maintain webs of trust as naturally as breathing; we have been the keepers of families, friendships, and communities forever. In business this is not a soft skill. It is the load-bearing wall.

**Diligence.** The unglamorous work of reading every page, checking every number, asking the awkward question. The same attention that catches a permission slip due Friday catches a clause that would have cost a partnership fifty thousand dollars.

**Relationships.** Deals travel through people: the neighbour who mentions a house before it lists, the mortgage broker who calls you first, the widow who will only sell to someone she likes. Women build these networks constantly and call it "just staying in touch."

**Judgment.** Decades of running a life teach pattern recognition no spreadsheet holds: which contractor is lying, which tenant is struggling versus scamming, which partner's charm has a smell to it. In my experience, a seasoned woman's read on people outperforms almost any model.

**Care.** The rarest one. Most people in any transaction are trying to get through it. The person who genuinely cares how it lands for everyone becomes the person everyone wants at their table. Care, at scale, is a business model. It has been mine for twenty-five years.

Notice what is on that list. Notice that not one item requires a finance degree, a six-figure account, or anyone's permission. You have been acquiring the scarce assets while apologizing for lacking the common one.

So here is your homework, and unlike the money, it cannot be borrowed. Take inventory. Honestly, on paper, tally what thirty or forty or fifty years of life have actually deposited in you. Then, and this is the hard part, practice saying it out loud without an apology attached. Not "I'm just really organized," but "I run diligence; nothing gets past me." Not "people seem to trust me for some reason," but "I keep promises, and my track record is my collateral."

The table is real, the seats are real, and one of them has been yours for years. The only thing missing has been your signature.

**Your Turn**

1. Score yourself, one to ten, on each of the six seats: money, credit, knowledge, time and effort, finding opportunities, and trust. Where are you strongest? Does your answer surprise you?
2. Write down your "invisible resume": five things you have managed, negotiated, saved, or rebuilt that never appeared on any CV.
3. Practice one sentence that states your strength without apology. Say it out loud until it stops feeling like bragging and starts feeling like fact.

# Chapter 10: Partners, Sisters, and Lemonade Again

Nothing I have built, I built alone. I want that sentence near the front of this chapter because our culture keeps selling women the self-made myth, and the myth is not just false. It is expensive. It keeps you waiting until you can do everything yourself, which is another way of saying it keeps you waiting forever.

My family crossed the river with partners. My first deal at twenty-one existed because partners filled the seats I could not. The portfolio that freed me by thirty was a braid of joint ventures, lenders, mentors, tradespeople, and one very patient sister. So let me tell you what twenty-five years of partnering has taught me, the beautiful and the painful, and then I will tell you about the lemonade.

**Choose character first, competence second, chemistry third.** A skilled partner without integrity is simply a sophisticated way to lose money. A delightful partner without follow-through is a slow way to lose it. Watch how a potential partner treats waiters, tenants, and people they outrank. Watch what they do when a small thing goes wrong. Small storms forecast big ones.

**Put everything in writing, especially with people you love.** Women resist this one hardest, because it feels like distrust. Hear me: paper is not distrust. Paper is kindness with a long memory. A clear written agreement about money, roles, decisions, and exits is how you guarantee that the deal, however it goes, will not cost you the relationship. The handshake protects nothing but the moment. My rule is simple: if we cannot discuss the agreement while we like each other, we certainly cannot discuss the disaster after we don't.

**Complement, don't duplicate.** The best partnerships pair differences: her capital with your hustle, her caution with your courage, her spreadsheet with your people-sense. If you and a potential partner bring identical strengths, you have doubled your blind spots, not your power.

**Start small on purpose.** Do a small thing together before a big thing. A tiny flip before a building. One event before a company. Partnership is a muscle you test with light weights first.

**And keep your own name on your own life.** Partnering is not merging. You can share a deal without surrendering your judgment, your signature, or your voice. The moment a partnership requires you to become smaller to keep the peace, the price has exceeded any possible return. I have walked away from money to keep myself. I have never once regretted it.

Now the lemonade.

Forty years after two little immigrant girls sold lemonade through the chain-link fence at the Shaganappi golf course, my sister and I stood side by side again. Canada Day, Kin Coulee Park, Medicine Hat. This time the stand had a name, Orchid Tap Bar, and a menu we had refined ourselves: nitro cold brew that sold out completely, nitro matcha we ran out of twice, and a strawberry-lemon creation called The Shag that stole the whole show. And working the booth beside us, laughing, pouring, learning to make change on a hot day, were our daughters.

Some dreams really do come full circle.

I stood there in the noise of the festival and cried a little, and I did not care who saw, because I could see all of it at once: the fence, the camp, our parents' hands, the decades of separate battles my sister and I had fought, and now our girls, third generation, learning through their fingertips the same lesson we learned through ours. Family is the first partnership. If you are lucky, and if you tend it, it can also be the longest.

That day was not about beverages, just as the fence was never about lemonade. It was about what two women can build when they stop waiting and start pouring. Every event since, we have improved something. That is the other secret of partnership, by the way: you do not have to launch perfectly. You have to launch together, and learn fast.

We are just getting started. So, I suspect, are you.

**Your Turn**

1. Think of the best team you have ever been part of, in any context. What made it work? What seat did you hold?
2. Who are three women you could realistically build something with? What would each of you bring? What small thing could you attempt together first?
3. Have you ever stayed small to keep a relationship peaceful? What did it cost? What would "keeping your own name on your own life" require you to change?
