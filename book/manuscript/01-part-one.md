# PART ONE: THE CROSSING

*Where strength begins*

# Chapter 1: The River

I was born in a country that no longer exists the way my parents knew it.

Laos, before the war ended, was my family's whole world: their language, their food, their ancestors, their plans. Then the communists took power, and the plans stopped mattering. My parents faced the kind of decision I hope you never have to face, though I suspect you have faced a version of it. Stay in a life that is slowly closing around you, or leave everything you know for a chance at something you cannot see yet.

They chose to leave.

I was too young to remember most of it myself. What I carry are my parents' memories, told and retold at our kitchen table over the years: the leaving, the fear, the border, and then the waiting. We spent more than a year in a refugee camp in Thailand. A year of lining up, of not knowing, of my mother and father holding a family together inside a fence with nothing to offer us but themselves.

Here is what I want you to understand about that year. My parents were not waiting passively. They were holding a decision. Every single day in that camp, they renewed the same choice: we are going somewhere better, and we are arriving there together. That is what courage actually looks like. It is rarely one dramatic moment. It is the same unglamorous choice, made again every morning, long after the adrenaline is gone.

In 1980, the miracle came in the most ordinary form imaginable: paperwork. A generous family and a church congregation in Canada, people who had never met us and owed us nothing, sponsored my family. Strangers signed their names for us. Because they did, I grew up in Calgary, Alberta, and I have now lived here for more than forty-five years.

I tell you this story first, before the real estate, before the deals, before any of it, for one reason.

Every woman I coach believes, somewhere deep down, that the successful women she admires started with something she does not have. More money. Better connections. A luckier family. A younger start. I need you to know that my starting line was a refugee camp, and that the first investors in my life were strangers from a church who decided a family they had never met was worth betting on.

You are not behind those women. You are simply earlier in your own story than they are in theirs.

And there is a second lesson hiding in the river crossing, one it took me decades to see. My parents did not cross alone. Nobody does. They were carried by boat pilots and border guides, by aid workers in the camp, by a sponsoring family and a church full of volunteers on the other side of the world. The bravest act of self-reliance in my family's history was also, from beginning to end, a partnership.

Hold onto that. It is the seed of everything this book will ask of you.

**Your Turn**

1. What "river" are you standing in front of right now: the change you know you need to make but have not yet committed to?
2. Courage is a daily renewal, not a single leap. What is one choice you could renew every morning for the next year?
3. Who has quietly "sponsored" you in your life so far? Who signed their name for you? What did that teach you about accepting help?

# Chapter 2: Lemonade Through the Fence

Everything in our house was homemade or secondhand.

I do not say that for sympathy. I say it because for years I was embarrassed by it, and now I understand it was the most valuable business education money could not buy. When nothing is handed to you, you learn to see resources where other people see nothing. You learn that between "we cannot afford it" and "we will figure it out" there is an entire universe, and your family lives or dies by which sentence they choose.

My parents chose "we will figure it out." Every time.

My favourite proof is a chain-link fence.

Our family lived near the Shaganappi golf course in Calgary. My sister and I were little girls with no allowance, no store, and no permission slip from anyone. What we had was a hot summer, a fence, and a steady supply of thirsty golfers walking past on the other side of it.

So we made lemonade, and we sold it through the fence.

Think about everything wrapped up inside that little enterprise. We found a customer with a need. We positioned ourselves exactly where the customer already was. We used the resources we had instead of waiting for the ones we wished for. We handled money, made change, managed inventory on a hot day, and split the take with a partner. Two immigrant kids ran the whole cycle of a business through the gaps in a chain-link fence, and we thought we were just playing.

Forty years later, I stood beside that same sister at a Canada Day festival in Medicine Hat, launching a real beverage company together, our daughters working the booth beside us. I will tell you that story properly near the end of this book, because it made me cry then and it makes me cry now. But I mention it here so you can see what I see: the fence was not a small thing. The fence was the first thing. Every big thing in my life started as a small thing that I refused to treat as small.

Now let me turn this toward you, because you have your own fence, and I suspect you cannot see it yet.

Women between thirty and sixty are the most resourceful human beings on this planet, and the least likely to give themselves credit for it. You run households on budgets that would terrify a CFO. You negotiate daily with toddlers, teenagers, contractors, schools, and aging parents. You coordinate schedules that look like air traffic control. You have been buying and selling, borrowing and lending, budgeting and forecasting your entire adult life. You just never called it business.

When a woman tells me, "Chan, I don't have any experience," what she almost always means is, "Nobody ever paid me a salary for the experience I have." Those are very different sentences.

Scarcity taught me creativity. Responsibility taught you management. Neither of us got the certificate, and neither of us needed it.

**Your Turn**

1. Write down three problems you solved in the last month without spending money. That is resourcefulness. Where else could it be pointed?
2. What was your "lemonade stand"? Somewhere in your past there is a moment you created value out of nothing. What did it feel like?
3. What skills do you use every week that you have never given yourself professional credit for? List five. Do not stop at three.

# Chapter 3: Home Is the People

My parents never had much money to give us, so they gave us beliefs instead. Looking back, I got the better end of that trade.

The first belief was gratitude. My parents taught me to love Canada, imperfections and all, the way you love family: honestly, loyally, and without pretending it is flawless. They never let us forget the government that opened the door in those years, or the family and the church who sponsored us. Whatever this country asked of us, we would give it, because we knew exactly what the alternative had been. Canada will always be in my heart. No matter what.

The second belief was unity. We did not have the newest anything. What we had, always, was each other. Through the camp, through the early years of being new and broke and foreign, through my parents' later health battles, the family held. My parents taught me that home is not a place. Home is the people.

I want to sit with that sentence, because it eventually became the foundation of my entire career, and I did not see it coming.

I have spent more than twenty-five years around real estate. Houses, duplexes, rentals, renovations, foreclosures, closings. From the outside it looks like an industry about buildings. It is not. I have handed keys to first-time buyers who cried in the driveway. I have sat at kitchen tables with families about to lose their homes to foreclosure and helped them find a way to leave with money in their pockets and their heads held high. I have watched empty rooms become the backdrop of someone's whole life.

Not once, in all those years, was the transaction really about the building. It was about the people inside it and what they were trying to protect, start, survive, or become.

This matters for you, and here is why.

Somewhere along the way, someone convinced women that ambition is selfish. That wanting money is unbecoming. That investing is what other people do, colder people, usually men in suits. So we hesitate, we defer, we wait for permission that never comes.

But if home is the people, then building wealth is not selfish at all. It is shelter-building. When I bought my first rental property, I was not collecting a trophy. I was making sure that what happened to my parents, arriving at midlife with nothing, could never happen to my children. Every property since has been another wall against the storm, for my family and for the tenant family living inside it.

Your reason will be your own. Your daughter's education. Your own dignity in retirement. The ability to leave a job, or a marriage, that is slowly making you smaller. The freedom to care for your parents the way they cared for you.

Find your people. Then build for them. Ambition rooted in love is not greed. It is the oldest, most honourable work there is.

**Your Turn**

1. Who are your people, the ones you are really building for? Name them. Write the names down where you will see them.
2. Complete this sentence honestly: "If money were not a limit, I would protect my people by..."
3. What belief about money did you inherit from your childhood home? Is it serving you, or do you need to hand it back with thanks?
